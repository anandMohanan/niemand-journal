I worked on microwave absorbers back in college. The design I studied had three seven-sided copper rings on a circuit-board substrate, with a copper layer underneath. Its simulations showed six absorption peaks across the S, C, X and Ku bands. I wrote about that work in a 2023 paper called *6 bands microwave metamaterial absorber for S, C, X, and Ku band applications*.

I recently came back to it with a different question: could AI help me improve the way I did this research? Could it help me choose what to test next, spot a bad assumption, and get more out of each simulation?

I used it to suggest experiments, write the scripts, and go through the results with me. The electromagnetic calculations ran in openEMS. I wanted to see whether that back-and-forth would lead to useful, testable ideas.

To give it a concrete problem, I picked something small: move one absorption peak without moving another.

I started with a simpler structure that had two peaks. I haven't reproduced the old six-band result here, so the numbers below are all from this new setup.

The first result looked pretty good. Adding a resistive film moved the lower peak up by about 10%, while the higher one barely moved. Then I tried carrying the idea over to three different shapes. None of them did what I wanted.

That sent me back to something I'd been treating as part of the setup: the walls.

## Starting with something I could check

I used openEMS to simulate a resistive film above a metal ground plane, with air between them. The spacer was 32 mm high, the film was 0.2 mm thick, and the simulated guide was 12 mm wide.

That last detail matters. I had electric-wall conditions on two sides and magnetic-wall conditions on the other two. These are boundaries imposed by the simulation. The results describe that particular guide; I haven't checked an open surface illuminated in free space.

Across 1–8 GHz, the reference had two absorption peaks, near **2.334 GHz and 7.000 GHz**. Having two gave me something to change and something to try to leave alone.

Before trying different patterns, I checked the simple layered structure against a separate transmission-line calculation. I also ran longer simulations, used three mesh resolutions, changed the distance to the boundaries along the propagation direction, and checked dissipated power from the saved fields. The fully checked cases had to pass those tests before I used them to judge a design.

I didn't do that full convergence check for every point in the larger prediction dataset. Those points passed the initial numerical checks, with selected cases getting the additional runs.

## A second film moved the lower peak

I added another resistive film one-third of the way up the air spacer. The original top film stayed in place.

The lower peak moved from roughly **2.334 to 2.569 GHz**, a **10.06% increase**. The upper peak moved from about **7.000 to 6.989 GHz**, a **0.16% decrease**, within the frequency uncertainty I was allowing for.

I also needed a control. Was this just what happened when I added more resistive material?

Putting the same additional film halfway up the spacer moved the lower peak by **14.20%**, but it also pulled the upper one down by **4.83%**. Same material and area, different position, different outcome.

Position was doing something useful. I still needed to find out how much of that would survive a different geometry.

## Three shapes, three failures

Next I replaced the added full-area film with an open U, an offset cross, and a slotted block. I scaled their sheet resistance with material area as a first attempt to preserve the loading. That was an assumption to test, not an exact electrical equivalence.

I recorded the predictions before running those new shape families. The goal stayed the same: raise the lower peak by about 10% and keep the upper peak within 2% of its original frequency.

| Added pattern | Lower peak shift | Upper peak shift |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| Open U | +0.68% | −1.58% |
| Offset cross | −0.29% | −2.97% |
| Slotted block | −0.43% | −4.92% |

None met the goal. The lower peak barely moved, and two patterns also moved the upper peak too far.

I couldn't carry the full-film result over just by adjusting for area. Something important was missing from that comparison.

## The film was touching the walls

The full film reached both electric walls. Those smaller patterns didn't.

I tested that difference with a straight resistive strip spanning the guide. For its control, I removed the end pieces and put the same amount of material inside the guide. Both patterns had **18 mm²** of material, the same sheet resistance, thickness, and height.

![Top views of the wall-spanning strip and its detached equal-area control. The blue vertical lines mark the two electric walls.](/images/microwave-peaks/wall-contact.svg)

*These are the actual pattern layouts used in the contact test. The detached pattern keeps the material area by moving the end pieces inward.*

Compared with the detached control, the connected strip shifted the lower peak up by **11.76%** and the upper one by **10.10%**. Both changes were larger than the estimated numerical uncertainty and the 2% effect threshold I'd set.

I tried a winding path too. Its connected-versus-detached comparison moved the lower peak by **13.54%**. I couldn't resolve a qualifying upper peak for the connected version inside the frequency window I'd fixed beforehand. I left that comparison unresolved rather than calling the missing peak a success.

There's still a limitation in the control: moving material away from the walls changes the shape and perimeter too. Matching area doesn't remove every geometric difference. I'd need more controls before assigning the whole effect to contact alone.

## The prediction ranges needed fixing too

Alongside the simulations, I had a script predicting where both peaks would land, with a range around each prediction.

An early check put every test result inside those ranges. That sounded reassuring until I looked at how wide they were. A bug wasn't restoring the predictions from standardized units. The ranges were so broad that containing everything meant very little.

I kept those results, fixed the conversion, and used fresh calibration and test cases. For a test to count, **both peaks had to fall inside their predicted ranges**. A missing qualifying pair counted as a failure too.

The corrected ranges were much narrower, but they missed the 90% target. They did particularly poorly on unfamiliar shapes. I couldn't treat a reasonable point prediction as evidence that its uncertainty range was reliable.

The search comparison was fairly modest too. Five search methods each got eight candidate queries on each of three new tuning problems. Every method found a fully verified solution on two of the three problems. The guided search tied the Bayesian and direct analytical methods on average objective loss. I didn't find an advantage over those stronger baselines.

## Where I left it

The campaign used **1,194 solver runs**, including empty references, mesh checks, controls and repetitions. That's quite a different number from 1,194 separate designs.

I have a useful position-dependent shift, a repeatable wall-contact effect, and a failed attempt to transfer the first result to other shapes. The prediction ranges still need work. None of this has been checked with fabrication measurements or a second full-wave solver.

On the original question, I have a working way to propose changes, test them, and follow up when they fail. I haven't shown that using AI makes the research faster or produces better designs than the alternatives. The search comparison didn't establish that advantage, and I didn't run a timed comparison against doing the work without it.

Next I'd like to separate wall contact from the other shape changes more carefully, then try a different fixture. Before I spend much more time choosing patterns, I want to know which parts of the response belong to the pattern and which depend on where I've put it.
